# Positioning Maps, Strengths and Weaknesses

> Owns: the price positioning decision (premium, parity, value) with proof, price-value maps, the strengths and weaknesses table and the positioning hypothesis in the report. Hands to `market-intel`: competitor research, VOC and review mining, perceptual surveys. Hands to `creative-strategy`: turning the positioning into messaging. Hands to `compliance`: every comparative or health related claim.

## 1. Price positions

| Position | Price index vs reference set | Needs | Risk |
|----------|------------------------------|-------|------|
| Premium | 115 to 150+ | Visible, provable superiority on attributes the segment values; brand trust | Volume ceiling; trading down in downturns |
| Parity plus | 100 to 115 | One or two clear advantages | Must keep the advantage visible |
| Parity | 95 to 105 | Comparable product, better availability or service | Price wars |
| Value | 80 to 95 | Lower cost to serve or simpler product | Margin pressure; quality doubts |
| Discount | under 80 | Structural cost advantage | Brand damage, channel conflict |

Choose the position per channel and segment; most brands sit at one position per category. Moving more than one band in a year confuses customers and retailers.

## 3. Maps

Map 1: Price per unit (y) vs perceived quality or value score (x). Value score sources: review rating, conjoint utilities, survey ratings, expert test. The diagonal is the "fair value line". Brands above the line are expensive for their value; below are bargains.

Map 2: Price per unit (y) vs a differentiating attribute (x), such as protein per bar, natural ingredient count, delivery speed, service hours. Shows clusters and white space.

Map 3 (channels): Per unit price by channel for each brand (retail shelf, DTC, marketplace). Shows who uses which channel at which price.

Deliver maps as tables when no chart tool is available:

| Brand | Price per unit | Value score | Attribute | Segment |
|-------|----------------|-------------|-----------|---------|

When a chart is produced, follow the `dataviz` skill.

## 4. Positioning hypothesis

Template: "We are [category frame] for [target customer] who value [top attribute]. Against [reference set], we win on [proof 1, proof 2] and lose on [weakness]. Our defensible price position is [position] at index [range] vs [reference set], communicated per [unit]."

Example structure (Amara pattern, illustrative): three clusters in the bar market: performance protein (high protein, gym), natural snack (simple ingredients, impulse), and a modern transparent nutrition position (organic, vegan, open about ingredients). The brand sits in the third. Hypothesis: "Organic vegan bar for health conscious adults who read labels. Wins on certification and transparency, loses on protein grams. Price at parity to +10% vs natural snack median per bar, below performance protein per bar; never compete on price per gram of protein."

Validation tests:
- Does WTP data support the index? ([Value based pricing](value-based-pricing-and-wtp.md))
- Does the shelf unit price (per 100 g) undermine the position? (lighter products look expensive per 100 g)
- Do retailers place the product next to the reference set we chose?

## 5. Strength to price translation

| Strength type | Typical price latitude | Note |
|---------------|------------------------|------|
| Certification (organic, B Corp) | Moderate premium in categories where it is valued | Measure with organic vs conventional variant prices on shelf |
| Functional performance (more protein, faster) | Premium per functional unit only if the customer counts that unit | |
| Convenience (delivery, subscription) | Covers delivery fee or a small premium | Easy to copy |
| Brand trust | Large in mature categories | Built over years |
| Service and guarantees | Supports premium in services and B2B | See offer-strategy guarantees |
| Availability (in every supermarket) | Usually parity pressure | Retail presence anchors price |

## 6. Common mistakes

1. Picking a premium position because the team likes the brand.
2. Comparing on the unit that favors competitors (per gram of protein when we are lighter on protein).
3. Ignoring private label as the floor reference on shelf.
4. Mixing segments with different reference sets into one map.
5. Positioning claims that are not in CLAIMS.md.

## 7. Building the value score (procedure)

1. Pick 4 to 6 attributes that the segment values (from reviews, search queries, surveys, sales calls).
2. Weight them (sum to 100) from customer evidence: survey importance ratings, conjoint utilities, or review mention frequency as a proxy.
3. Score each brand 1 to 5 per attribute with a written rule per score (for example protein: 1 = under 8 g, 3 = 10 to 14 g, 5 = 20 g or more).
4. Value score = sum(weight x score) / 5. Record sources per score.
5. Plot price per unit against value score. Fit a simple line through competitors (the fair value line).
6. Distance above or below the line is the price premium or discount relative to value.

Worked layout (illustrative weights and scores, replace with evidence):

| Brand | Price per bar | Organic (25) | Clean label (25) | Taste (25) | Protein (15) | Availability (10) | Value score |
|-------|---------------|--------------|------------------|------------|--------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Us | 2.50 (DTC hero) | 5 | 5 | 4 | 2 | 2 | 80 |
| Competitor A (performance) | [P_A] | 1 | 2 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 60 |
| Competitor B (protein value) | [P_B] | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 50 |
| Competitor C (natural snack) | [P_C] | 3 | 4 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 66 |

Value score = (25 x 5 + 25 x 5 + 25 x 4 + 15 x 2 + 10 x 2) / 5 = 80 for us. Weights decide the map; a gym segment weights protein at 40 and the map flips. Build one map per segment.

## 8. Competitor response matrix

Price moves invite responses. Before recommending a position change, fill in:

| Competitor | Likely response to our move | Capacity to respond (margin, scale) | History (promo frequency, past reactions) | Our counter |
|-----------|----------------------------|--------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|-------------|
| A | | | | |
| B | | | | |
| Retailer private label | | | | |

Rules: big brands with retail promo calendars respond through promotions, not list prices; private label responds slowly; DTC newcomers often respond with codes. A premium position rarely triggers a response; a value move into a competitor's zone often does.

## 9. Positioning section checklist (report)

- [ ] Segment and reference set named.
- [ ] Strengths and weaknesses with proof and importance.
- [ ] At least one map with per unit prices dated.
- [ ] Positioning hypothesis in the template form.
- [ ] Index range tied to the position band.
- [ ] Communication unit chosen (per bar, per 100 g, per serving) and the reason.
- [ ] Claims to verify listed for `compliance`.
- [ ] Competitor response matrix filled for any move of more than one band.

## 10. Positioning by business model

| Model | What positions price | Typical reference set |
|-------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| Ecommerce DTC with retail | Retail shelf price of own and adjacent brands | Category shelf |
| Marketplace seller | Buy box price and top 3 sellers | Same listing and close substitutes |
| SaaS | Competitor tiers per seat or value metric | Direct tools and the spreadsheet or manual alternative |
| Local services | Local competitors' quotes and public price lists | 3 to 5 nearby providers |
| B2B wholesale | Competing suppliers' list and pocket prices | Supplier panel of the buyer |
| App | Store category price tiers and subscription norms | Top grossing apps in category |
